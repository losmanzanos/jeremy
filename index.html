The mark, as it stands
I · The Mark

The apex of the body is a folded landscape.

Look at a brain from above and you’re looking at a contour map. That isn’t a metaphor anyone reached for. A brain folds for the same reason a mountain range does, to pack more surface into the space available. Draw one honestly and you’ve drawn the other.

The mark carries both readings at once. Cortex on one side, and on the other the network those folds go on to carry: the nodes, the routes, the paths worn in by repetition.

II · Why Apex

Apex means the highest point. You are carrying one.

The brain is the body’s. It’s the summit every other system is built to supply, with blood and oxygen and sleep all running uphill to keep one organ lit.

Colorado is the country’s. More peaks above fourteen thousand feet than any other state, and the highest mean elevation of the fifty. The highest ground in America and the highest ground in a person, both mapped the same way: by tracing the lines where the elevation holds steady.

What apex has never meant is the summit photograph. Ask anyone who has moved here from sea level. You don’t conquer altitude, you acclimatize to it, and the body takes several weeks to manage it.

The elevation that flattened you in August becomes the one you simply live at by October. That is the closer description of this work. Not a peak to reach and post from, but the capacity to hold height in the life you already have.

Not a peak to reach. The capacity to hold height.

Whether or not you’ve said it out loud yet. No lecture and no script, just an honest look at what it is costing you and what you actually want instead.

04

Physicians, founders, attorneys, executives. Being good at your job is not the same as having anything left over, and the goal you finally reach doesn’t always fix what you were sure it would.

05

Anxiety, depression, and anger

The worry that will not switch off. The flatness that shows up once it finally does. The temper that arrives faster than you’d like and costs you afterward.

06

Couples

Two people who used to understand each other, trying to find the way back.

07

Grief, faith, and life transitions

Loss. Belief that has shifted. A chapter that closed before you were ready for it to.

VI · Questions Asked before a first call

The things people want to know first.

Do you take my insurance?
Aetna, Anthem Blue Cross and Blue Shield, Carelon, Cigna and Select Health are all in network, and those sessions are booked through Headway, which shows you your exact cost before you confirm anything. Covered somewhere else? You can still be seen, and I will give you a receipt each month to send to your insurer. Ask on the first call and you will get a straight answer about what this actually costs you.
What happens in a first session?
We spend the hour on a fairly thorough conversation about what brought you here, what you have already tried, and what you would like to be different. It is semi-structured, so I will ask questions and keep us moving, but you decide what we get into and what keeps for another day. By the end we will both have a sense of whether this is a good fit.
How long does this usually take?
It depends on what you are bringing. Some people come for eight or ten sessions with something specific in mind. Others stay longer because the work opens up. I will not keep you in therapy you no longer need, and if you ever want an honest read on how it is going, just ask me.
Is online therapy really as good as being in the room?
For most adults and most concerns the research says yes, and that includes trauma work and EMDR. It also removes the drive, the parking and the waiting room, which is often the difference between going every week and quietly stopping. It is not the right fit for an acute crisis, and if that is where you are, I will tell you and help you find the right level of care.
Do you prescribe medication?
No. I am a licensed professional counselor, not a psychiatrist. If medication seems worth considering I will say so, and I can coordinate with your doctor or point you toward a prescriber who takes your insurance.
I have never done therapy before. Is that a problem?
Not at all, and it is more common than you would think, particularly among people who are used to being the one everyone else leans on. You do not need the right words or a tidy explanation of what is wrong. Showing up and saying that something feels off is plenty to start with.
Where does faith fit in?
Wherever you want it to. I am trained to work with questions of meaning, belief and doubt, and I am glad to sit in them with you. I am equally glad never to raise the subject at all. It is your hour, and it follows your lead.
